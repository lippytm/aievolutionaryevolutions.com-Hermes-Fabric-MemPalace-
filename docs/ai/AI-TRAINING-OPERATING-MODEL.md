# AI Teaching, Training, and Application Operating Model

AI is used throughout the ecosystem as a teaching assistant, development partner, reviewer, operator, and improvement engine. Its behavior must remain transparent, bounded, and measurable.

## Training layers

### Knowledge training

Teach concepts through structured lessons, examples, glossaries, references, simulations, and practical projects.

### Skill training

Teach repeatable procedures through guided labs, checklists, sandboxes, tests, and progressively harder challenges.

### Systems training

Teach how components interact across code, APIs, databases, Linux, networks, robotics, blockchain, security, and business systems.

### Judgment training

Teach tradeoffs, uncertainty, failure analysis, ethics, safety, privacy, and when to request human review.

### Operational training

Teach monitoring, incident response, documentation, release management, backups, rollback, and continuous improvement.

## AI training quick actions

- Generate a lesson or learning path.
- Explain a concept at a selected difficulty.
- Create a guided lab.
- Generate exercises and assessments.
- Review a learner's solution.
- Provide hints without revealing the full solution.
- Convert documentation into training content.
- Simulate an incident or design review.
- Create a secure coding challenge.
- Compare multiple implementation approaches.
- Identify knowledge gaps.
- Recommend the next skill.
- Create a progress report.
- Update training content after a verified change.

## Quality rules

Training outputs must be accurate for their stated version, clear about uncertainty, accessible, safe to practice, and reviewed before publication when they concern security, finance, health, production infrastructure, or irreversible operations.

AI must distinguish facts, assumptions, examples, simulations, and recommendations. It must not present unverified generated code or security procedures as production-ready.

## Continuous improvement

Learning feedback, assessment results, incidents, support findings, and approved engineering outcomes may inform future content. Changes must be versioned, reviewed, evaluated against prior material, and traceable to their source or reason.
